Software factory experiment: final report

23 November 2026. The question was which way of working gives you pull requests you can trust, for the least of your attention and money. About 370 graded runs over five rounds, on your own app and two open-source projects. This page gives the answer to each of the charter's questions, with the things you can now use.

What you can do with this tomorrow

  1. Give the agent a standard ticket, not a one-liner. A few hundred words: the problem, why, what is in and out, "done when". On a rough one-line capture a bare run was trustworthy on 1 ticket of 7; on the standard ticket, 5 of 7.
  2. Work it with the winning checklist on Opus 5.5. A 168-word guide and six small skills, two of which the guide points to: check the ask against the code before building, and prove each claim before reporting. It is packaged in checklist/ for you to review and install. Nothing has been installed.
  3. Say "stop and ask me about X" in the ticket for anything undecided, and name any decision record the change touches. Those are the two places it still fails. This item is my advice from what the runs did; the wording itself was not tested.
  4. Skip the heavy machinery for single tickets. It cost more money each time, often more of your attention too, and never bought more trust.

Still yours to decide, none urgent: installing the checklist (it has never run as an installed plugin); whether I may delete about 166 GB of run folders; the optional Astra judge login; and a fairer rule for the "edited an existing test" trap if the kit is used again.

No, but the ticket matters more than anything else tested. A bare run on a full spec matched the guide on a standard ticket (6.9 against 6.9 of 8), but you would read more, so by the rule it does not replace the guide. Neither a fuller spec nor acceptance tests fixed the wrong request. Script

Where it still fails

  • A request that quietly breaks a decision recorded somewhere else. On your app's version of this, 0 of 15 runs caught it with a standard ticket and no pointed skills. With the skill pointed to, it was raised in 5 runs of 5. Name the decision in the ticket and you do not depend on the skill.
  • An open point the agent can step around. When the work could not go on without your answer, runs asked. When a ticket only said "not yet decided" and the rest was buildable, none of six asked. Write "stop and ask me".
  • Untrue statements in the report. With the code right, this is most of what remains: 5 of the winner's 56 runs, 3 of them on a point that matters. The skill for proving claims did not measurably reduce them: on the same eight tickets, 2 runs of 24 with it and 2 of 20 without.

How far to trust this

  • The tickets were short. Runs averaged 6 to 18 minutes for a single agent, 32 for the team. Whether the light method holds on day-long work was not tested, and by stage 4 every setup was near the top of the test, which then cannot rank them.
  • Few runs per ticket. One in stage 1, three for the main comparison, two elsewhere. Differences under one pull request in eight are noise, and the rules mostly treated them so. Once they did not: the stage 2 rule turned on 0.06 of a quality point, which is why the tie-break round was run.
  • A model judges honesty, and it is not perfectly repeatable. Judging the earlier stages again changed 3 verdicts in 80 and no decision. Two judges agreed on about 93 runs in 100.
  • Every stage had flaws in its own grading, each logged when found and corrected afterwards, with both numbers kept. The largest: a hidden test that rejected a correct answer (stage 1), a key that punished a correct fix (stage 2), and a judge that mixed up a lead and its worker (stage 4).
  • Answers were within reach in the early stages. Temp folders were readable by runs until stage 4. A search of all 277 earlier transcripts found no run that opened one. Script A program reading silently cannot be ruled out. Not checked
  • It will age. One Claude Code version and one model generation. Treat it as suspect after a new model or about a month.
